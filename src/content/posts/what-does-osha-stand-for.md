OSHA stands for the **Occupational Safety and Health Administration**. It is a United States federal agency that sets and enforces workplace safety and health standards, and it is part of the [US Department of Labor](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha).

This guide covers US OSHA only. Other countries have their own regulators and laws, so if you work outside the United States, check your national authority.

## What does OSHA stand for?

The letters stand for Occupational Safety and Health Administration. 'Occupational' means work-related, so the name refers to safety and health at work. 'Administration' is the US government term for the agency itself.

### Is it 'Occupational Health Safety Administration' or 'Occupational Safety and Health Administration'?

The correct name is Occupational Safety and Health Administration. [OSHA's own pages](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha) and the [USAGov agency listing](https://www.usa.gov/agencies/occupational-safety-and-health-administration) both use that wording. 'Occupational Health Safety Administration' is a common mix-up of the word order, not the agency's name.

## What is OSHA, and what is the OSH Act?

The two are often confused, but they are different things.

- **The OSH Act** is the law. According to OSHA, [the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 created OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/workers).
- **OSHA** is the agency. A [NIOSH-provided description](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/226356) says Congress passed the Act in 1970 to establish a national system for workplace safety and health standards development, standards enforcement and consultative assistance, administered by OSHA in the US Department of Labor.

OSHA's administrator is the Assistant Secretary of Labor for Occupational Safety and Health, who [answers to the Secretary of Labor](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha), a member of the President's cabinet.

The statute also created a separate body for disputes. It provides for [an Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact) to carry out adjudicatory functions under the Act.

## What is OSHA's mission and purpose?

OSHA's current mission statement, from its [About OSHA page](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha), is to ensure America's workers have safe and healthful working conditions free from unlawful retaliation.

Two details are worth knowing:

- **The 'unlawful retaliation' phrase is part of the current wording.** Older write-ups of the mission, including some third-party pages that quote earlier text, may leave it out. Use the osha.gov version.
- **osha.gov wording varies slightly.** The [careers page](https://www.osha.gov/careers) says 'assure' where the About page says 'ensure'. The rest of the statement is the same.

According to OSHA, it carries out the mission by:

1. setting and enforcing standards;
2. enforcing the anti-retaliation provisions of the OSH Act and other federal whistleblower laws;
3. providing and supporting training, outreach, education and assistance; and
4. working with state OSHA programs and ensuring they are at least as effective as federal OSHA.

The purpose Congress wrote into the law is broader in tone. In the [Act's findings and purpose section](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact), Congress says it wants to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions and to preserve human resources. Among the listed means: authorizing the Secretary of Labor to set mandatory standards, providing for research and training programs, and encouraging joint labor-management efforts to reduce injuries and disease.

## What does OSHA do?

In practice, OSHA's work falls into a few areas.

### Sets standards

According to OSHA, there are [standards for construction, agriculture, maritime and general industry](https://www.osha.gov/workers). Employers must also comply with the General Duty Clause of the OSH Act, which requires them to keep workplaces free of serious recognized hazards.

### Enforces them

OSHA's [enforcement page](https://www.osha.gov/enforcement) says it sets enforcement policy and targeted inspection programs, and responds to fatalities, catastrophes and complaints. The Act itself includes [a prohibition on giving advance notice of any inspection](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact).

### Helps employers comply

The same enforcement page lists compliance assistance, including compliance assistance specialists and an On-Site Consultation Program. OSHA also lists data tools such as the top 10 most frequently cited standards.

### Protects whistleblowers

OSHA enforces anti-retaliation provisions. Its worker page says it is [illegal for an employer to fire, demote, transfer, or otherwise retaliate](https://www.osha.gov/workers) against a worker for complaining to OSHA.

This guide does not cover penalty amounts, injury recordkeeping rules or reporting deadlines for fatalities and hospitalizations. Get those from OSHA directly.

## Who does OSHA cover, and what are state plans?

OSHA says the OSH Act [covers most private sector employers and their workers](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha), plus some public sector employers and workers, in the 50 states and certain territories and jurisdictions under federal authority. Those listed are the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Wake Island, Johnston Island, and the Outer Continental Shelf Lands as defined in the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act.

'Some public sector' matters: coverage is not identical for every kind of employer, so confirm your own situation with OSHA.

### State plans

The OSH Act encourages [the States to assume the fullest responsibility for administering and enforcing their own occupational safety and health laws](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact), and provides grants to help. OSHA says it works with its [state OSHA programs](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha) and makes sure they are at least as effective as federal OSHA.

In practice, your nearest office may be a federal or a State Plan office. OSHA's worker page tells you to [find your nearest federal or State Plan office](https://www.osha.gov/workers). Details of a state's rules can differ, so check with your state program if one applies to you.

## What do employers and workers have to do under the OSH Act?

The Act says employers and employees have [separate but dependent responsibilities and rights](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact) for achieving safe and healthful working conditions.

**Employers.** OSHA says your employer [must keep the workplace free from known safety and health hazards](https://www.osha.gov/workers). The OSH Act also imposes duties on employers to comply with OSHA's standards [under a system of civil and criminal sanctions](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/226356).

**Workers.** OSHA lists a number of worker rights, including:

- training in a language you understand;
- required safety equipment, such as gloves or a harness and lifeline for falls;
- requesting an OSHA inspection and speaking to the inspector;
- reviewing records of work-related injuries and illnesses; and
- speaking up or reporting concerns without being punished or treated unfairly.

For what personal protective equipment means, see [What Does PPE Stand For?](/blog/what-does-ppe-stand-for). For head protection, see [Hard Hat Types and Classes](/blog/hard-hat-types-and-classes).

**Example:** A warehouse employee reports a hazard to OSHA and is later demoted. OSHA's worker page says a whistleblower complaint can be filed [within 30 days of the retaliation](https://www.osha.gov/workers). That clock runs from the retaliation, so the employee should not wait.

## ¿Qué es OSHA?

OSHA son las siglas en inglés de Occupational Safety and Health Administration (en español, literalmente, Administración de Seguridad y Salud Ocupacional). Es una agencia federal de Estados Unidos, parte del Departamento de Trabajo, que establece y hace cumplir normas de seguridad y salud en el trabajo. Esta guía trata solo de la OSHA de EE. UU.; en otros países hay organismos distintos. OSHA indica en su [página sobre la agencia](https://www.osha.gov/aboutosha) que ofrece materiales en español.

## Where can I find OSHA's rules and contact my local office?

- **Law:** the [OSH Act text on osha.gov](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/completeoshact). That page covers amendments through January 1, 2004, so check current law before relying on it for anything beyond the points above.
- **Phone:** [1-800-321-6742](https://www.usa.gov/agencies/occupational-safety-and-health-administration) (1-800-321-OSHA).
- **Office:** use the 'find your nearest federal or State Plan office' link on the [worker rights page](https://www.osha.gov/workers).
- **Questions:** the same page has an 'Ask OSHA a question' option.

## What to check at your own workplace

1. **Find out who has jurisdiction.** Ask your employer, or check with OSHA, whether you fall under federal OSHA or a State Plan, and whether you are a private or public employer.
2. **Look for the basics.** Ask whether you have training in a language you understand, and whether required safety equipment is provided.
3. **Raise concerns internally first, where you can.** OSHA suggests telling your employer about a problem, since they may fix it quickly. You can still file a confidential complaint if you think the workplace is unsafe.
4. **Know the retaliation window.** A whistleblower complaint must be filed within 30 days of the retaliation.
5. **Ask a competent person for site-specific rules.** For standards that apply to your industry, contact OSHA or your State Plan office, or ask your employer's safety lead.
