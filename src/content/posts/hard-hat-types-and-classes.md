Hard hat types (Type I, Type II) and classes (G, E, C) are categories in the ANSI Z89.1 industrial head protection standard. They are not written into OSHA's rule. OSHA's construction rule, [29 CFR 1926.100](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.100), requires head protection that meets one of three named ANSI Z89.1 editions, and adds an electrical insulation requirement for high-voltage exposure.

One limit on this guide: the sources behind it are OSHA and eCFR pages. They do not reproduce the ANSI Z89.1 text, so this guide does not define what each type or class letter means or give class voltages. For those, read the standard itself or the manufacturer's documentation for your model.

## Quick answer: what OSHA's hard hat rule says

| Question | What 1926.100 says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Who is covered? | Employees working where there is possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock and burns | [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.100) |
| Which standard? | ANSI Z89.1-2009, Z89.1-2003 or Z89.1-1997 | [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-E/section-1926.100) |
| High-voltage exposure? | Must also meet Section 9.7 (Electrical Insulation) of one of those editions | [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-E/section-1926.100) |
| Alternative? | A device the employer demonstrates is at least as effective is deemed compliant | [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.100) |
| Type II, chin strap, replacement interval? | Not stated in the text | [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-E/section-1926.100) |

This covers US federal OSHA, construction only. State-plan states may have their own rules, and other countries use different standards.

## What are the hard hat types and classes, and where do they come from?

Types and classes sit in the consensus standard that OSHA points to, ANSI Z89.1, titled "American National Standard for Industrial Head Protection" in the 2009 and 2003 editions. The OSHA text never uses the words Type or Class. It only says the head protection must meet the standard's specifications.

That has a practical consequence. Some vendor pages say Type II or a chin strap is "required" by OSHA. The rule text does not say that. Whether a particular type or class suits your job is a hazard-assessment decision, backed by the standard and the manufacturer's data, not something 1926.100 dictates by name.

A hat's label is where you find its type and class markings. Read them against the standard or the manufacturer's documentation rather than a retailer's description. Brim style (full brim or cap style) is a separate feature from type, and OSHA's text does not address it.

## What do OSHA's hard hat rules and requirements actually say?

The full operative text of 1926.100 is short. In plain terms:

1. **Paragraph (a):** employees working in areas with a possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock and burns "shall be protected by protective helmets."
2. **Paragraph (b)(1):** the employer must provide each employee with head protection meeting ANSI Z89.1-2009, Z89.1-2003 or Z89.1-1997.
3. **Paragraph (b)(2):** for employees exposed to high-voltage electric shock and burns, the head protection must also meet Section 9.7 ("Electrical Insulation") of one of those editions.
4. **Paragraph (b)(3):** OSHA deems compliant any device the employer demonstrates is at least as effective as one built to a listed edition.

The duty to provide the head protection sits with the employer. The same text is on [OSHA's site](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.100) and in the [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-E/section-1926.100).

## Which ANSI Z89.1 edition does OSHA accept?

Only the three above: 2009, 2003 and 1997. The eCFR page shows the section was last amended in 2012 ([77 FR 37600, June 22, 2012; 77 FR 42988, July 23, 2012](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1926/subpart-E/section-1926.100)) and reports no changes to it after 1/03/2017.

You may see newer editions of Z89.1 on labels or in vendor pages, including a Z89.1-2026 edition. I could not confirm any newer edition, or any OSHA statement on accepting it, from the sources used here, so OSHA's listed editions may lag the current standard. If your hats are marked to a newer edition, the safe route is to ask the manufacturer for documentation showing the model also meets a listed edition, or to use the (b)(3) route of demonstrating it is at least as effective. Ask OSHA or a competent person if unsure.

## When must workers wear a hard hat or safety helmet?

When there is a possible danger of head injury from any of the hazards in paragraph (a). The text says "protective helmets"; hard hat is the usual name for them.

| Hazard named in 1926.100(a) | Example situation (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Impact | Moving around low structures or equipment |
| Falling or flying objects | Work under overhead activity |
| Electrical shock and burns | Work near energized parts |

The examples are mine, not OSHA's. Whether a task counts as a possible danger is a judgment for the employer's hazard assessment.

## Are vented or cool hard hats allowed near electrical hazards?

The rule text does not mention vented hats. What it does require is that head protection for employees exposed to high-voltage electric shock and burns meets Section 9.7 (Electrical Insulation). So the question is whether the specific model is documented as meeting that section. Vendor claims that vented means no electrical rating are not backed by a source here. Check the label and the manufacturer's data for the exact model before using it near energized equipment.

## How do hard hats differ from bump caps?

The sources do not discuss bump caps. The test that matters under OSHA is whether the head protection meets one of the named ANSI Z89.1 editions (or is shown to be at least as effective). A product not marked or documented that way is not shown to comply, whatever it is called.

## How often should hard hats be inspected and replaced? Is training required?

1926.100 contains no inspection frequency, no replacement interval and no training requirement. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for inspection, service life and replacement. Do not use a hat the instructions say to retire.

On training, the construction standards list separate sections for [safety training and education (1926.21) and personal protective equipment (1926.28)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926). I have only seen their titles, so read them before deciding what your training must cover. For PPE basics, see [what PPE stands for](/blog/what-does-ppe-stand-for).

## Example: choosing hats for a mixed crew

For example, a contractor has a crew doing general site work and two employees working where they may be exposed to high-voltage electric shock and burns. Under 1926.100, all employees need head protection meeting Z89.1-2009, -2003 or -1997. The two exposed employees need hats that also meet Section 9.7 of one of those editions. The contractor would ask the manufacturer for documentation for those models. The rule does not set a different type for the general crew.

## What should I check at my own workplace?

- Confirm which rule applies. This guide covers 29 CFR 1926.100 for construction; general industry and state-plan states may differ.
- Check each hat's label and manufacturer documentation for the ANSI Z89.1 edition it meets, and compare with the 2009, 2003 and 1997 list.
- For anyone exposed to high-voltage electric shock and burns, get documentation that the model meets Section 9.7 (Electrical Insulation).
- If you use a newer standard edition or an unlisted product, record how you demonstrate it is at least as effective.
- Keep the manufacturer's inspection and replacement instructions with your PPE records.
- Confirm what training applies under the separate OSHA sections.
- When in doubt, ask your OSHA area office, state-plan regulator or a competent person.
