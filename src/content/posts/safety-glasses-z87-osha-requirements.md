Under federal OSHA rules, safety glasses are eye protection that complies with one of three ANSI Z87.1 editions listed in [29 CFR 1910.133(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-I/section-1910.133), or that the employer can show is at least as effective. They must be marked to identify the manufacturer. Where flying objects are a hazard, they must also give side protection. Ordinary eyeglasses are not one of the rule's options.

General industry employers follow 29 CFR 1910.133, and construction employers follow [29 CFR 1926.102](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.102). Since [OSHA's 2016 final rule](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/federalregister/2016-03-25-0), the core duties in the two rules use the same wording. The eCFR text used for this guide was up to date as of 11/09/2026 and shows no changes to 1910.133 after 1/03/2017.

| Question | Federal answer | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who has the duty? | The employer, for each affected employee exposed to eye or face hazards | [1910.133(a)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-I/section-1910.133), 1926.102 |
| Which standards count? | ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2010, ANSI Z87.1-2003, ANSI Z87.1-1989 (R-1998), or a device the employer shows is equally effective | 1910.133(b)(1)–(2) |
| Side shields? | Required when flying objects are a hazard; detachable clip-on or slide-on shields are acceptable if they meet the section's requirements | 1910.133(a)(2) |
| Prescription glasses? | Rx built into the eye protection, or protection worn over the glasses | 1910.133(a)(3) |
| Marking? | Distinctly marked to identify the manufacturer | 1910.133(a)(4) |
| Welding or cutting light? | Filter lenses with a shade number suited to the work | 1910.133(a)(5) |
| Dust or chemical splash? | Guidance: fit the device so it seals to the face | [Appendix B, paragraph 6](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910SubpartIAppB) (nonmandatory) |

## What are safety glasses, and how are they different from regular glasses?

Safety glasses are protective devices made to a consensus standard that OSHA recognizes. Regular glasses only correct vision. OSHA describes ANSI Z87.1 as setting criteria for "description, general requirements, testing, marking, selection, care, and use" of protectors against hazards such as impact, non-ionizing radiation and chemical injuries ([OSHA eye and face protection standards page](https://www.osha.gov/eye-face-protection/standards)).

OSHA's Appendix B calls safety glasses "spectacles." [NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ppe/eye-safety/index.html) lists spectacles alongside goggles, face shields and full facepiece respirators as personal protective eyewear. For how eyewear fits into PPE in general, see [what PPE stands for](/blog/what-does-ppe-stand-for).

## When does OSHA require safety glasses at work?

OSHA requires eye or face protection whenever an employee is exposed to eye or face hazards from flying particles, molten metal, liquid chemicals, acids or caustic liquids, chemical gases or vapors, or potentially injurious light radiation. That is the wording of [1910.133(a)(1)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.133), and 1926.102 repeats it for construction.

The duty follows the hazard, not the job title. Appendix B lists jobs where eye protection "should be routinely considered" and says the list is not complete. It includes carpenters, electricians, machinists, plumbers and pipe fitters, sanders, grinding machine operators, sawyers, welders, laborers, chemical process operators and timber cutting and logging workers. NIOSH says small particles such as dust, cement chips, metal slivers and wood chips cause most eye injuries.

### How the hazard assessment decides the eyewear

[Appendix B](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910SubpartIAppB) is OSHA's nonmandatory guidance on assessing hazards and choosing PPE. It sets out these steps:

1. Walk through the area and identify hazard sources.
2. Organize the information you gathered.
3. Estimate the type, level of risk and seriousness of each hazard, including exposure to several hazards at once.
4. Compare the hazards (impact velocities, masses, projectile shape, radiation intensities) with what the available equipment can do.
5. Pick equipment that gives a level of protection "greater than the minimum required."
6. Fit each user and explain care, use, warning labels and limitations.

## What does the Z87 or Z87+ marking on safety glasses mean?

The marking requirement in OSHA's rule is narrow. [1910.133(a)(4)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-I/section-1910.133) only requires eye and face PPE to be "distinctly marked to facilitate identification of the manufacturer." The Z87 and Z87+ marks themselves are defined in ANSI Z87.1, a paid standard. The federal rule text doesn't explain them, and this guide doesn't reproduce the standard.

The [2016 Federal Register notice](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/federalregister/2016-03-25-0) said the 2010, 2003 and 1989 (R-1998) editions could be bought from ANSI as a single PDF package for $57. To find out what a mark means on a specific product, check the manufacturer's documentation. Appendix B says manufacturer's instructions should be "followed carefully" where they are available.

### What "impact-resistant" means under the federal rule

OSHA's rule text doesn't set its own impact rating. Impact performance comes from the Z87.1 edition the eyewear complies with. Appendix B asks you to match impact velocity, mass and projectile shape against what the eyewear can withstand. Note (10) of the Appendix B chart says spectacles without side shields give frontal protection only and are not acceptable for the "impact" sources and operations in the chart.

## Which ANSI Z87.1 editions does OSHA accept?

[1910.133(b)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-I/section-1910.133) lists three editions, and 1926.102(b)(1) lists the same three:

| Edition listed in the rule | Title as listed |
| --- | --- |
| ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2010 | Occupational and Educational Personal Eye and Face Protection Devices |
| ANSI Z87.1-2003 | Occupational and Educational Personal Eye and Face Protection Devices |
| ANSI Z87.1-1989 (R-1998) | Practice for Occupational and Educational Eye and Face Protection |

The 2016 final rule (81 FR 16090, March 25, 2016) added the 2010 edition and removed the oldest one, Z87.1-1989 (the original version, not the 1998 reaffirmation). It also replaced the construction rule's reference to Z87.1-1968. According to the [2016 notice](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/federalregister/2016-03-25-0), employers could adopt the 2010 edition "if they choose and at their convenience" or keep following the 2003 or 1989 (R-1998) editions.

If eyewear was made to an edition not on this list, the only other route is 1910.133(b)(2). Under (b)(2), a device complies if the employer demonstrates it is at least as effective as one built to a listed edition. OSHA's [interpretation index for 1910.133](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/interlinking/standards/1910.133/standard_interpretations) includes letters applying (b)(2) to specific products.

## Do safety glasses need side shields?

Yes, whenever flying objects are a hazard. [1910.133(a)(2)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.133) requires eye protection with side protection in that case and accepts detachable side protectors (for example, clip-on or slide-on side shields) that meet the section's requirements.

OSHA's standards page notes that the 1998 revision of Z87.1 recognized a Bureau of Labor Statistics study showing that workers need angular protection as well as frontal protection. OSHA has answered questions on this point in letters dated 12/09/1976 (clip-on side shields) and 01/29/2007 (detachable vs. permanent side shields and modifications to safety glasses), both listed in its [interpretation index](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/interlinking/standards/1910.133/standard_interpretations).

## Can I wear my prescription glasses as safety glasses?

Your everyday prescription glasses only count if they are protective eyewear with the prescription built in. [1910.133(a)(3)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-I/section-1910.133) allows two options for employees who wear prescription lenses during operations with eye hazards:

1. Eye protection that incorporates the prescription in its design.
2. Eye protection worn over the prescription lenses "without disturbing the proper position of the prescription lenses or the protective lenses."

Note (5) of Appendix B says the same thing in plain terms. The 2016 rule replaced the construction rule's corrective-lens wording with the wording from 1910.133(a)(3), so the two options apply on construction sites too. For electrical work, Appendix B note (7) advises caution with metal-frame protective devices in electrical hazard areas. OSHA also has a 02/04/2013 letter on updating prescription safety glasses.

## Safety glasses or goggles: which do you need in a laboratory?

Lab tasks where acids, caustics or other liquids can splash usually need goggles, or another face-sealing protector, rather than open spectacles. The sources used here don't include an eyewear rule written specifically for labs. The decision therefore follows the hazard-based duty in 1910.133(a)(1), whose list of hazards includes liquid chemicals, acids or caustic liquids, and chemical gases or vapors.

Appendix B gives three pointers for labs:

- Paragraph 6: protection against dust and chemical splash should be fitted so the device is sealed to the face. Spectacles with side shields don't seal.
- Note (11): eyewear should be ventilated, but the vents must keep splashes out.
- Note (8): restricted ventilation can make lenses fog, and frequent cleaning may be needed.

Where a face shield is added for larger pours, note (3) says it should only be worn over spectacles or goggles. Note (1) says to protect against the highest level of each hazard present. NIOSH adds that eye infections can spread through blood splashes and droplets from coughing or sneezing, which matters in clinical and biological labs.

## How do you choose safety glasses for the hazard?

Match the eyewear to the hazard your assessment found, then check fit. This table combines the rule text with OSHA's nonmandatory guidance. The guidance columns are advice, not legal requirements.

| Hazard | What the rule requires | What the guidance adds | Usual choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flying particles (grinding, sawing, chipping) | Side protection, 1910.133(a)(2) | Note (10): no-side-shield spectacles are not acceptable for impact; one chart entry adds "For severe exposure, use faceshield." | Spectacles with side protection or goggles, with a face shield over them for severe exposure |
| Liquid chemicals, acids, caustics | Appropriate eye or face protection, 1910.133(a)(1) | Paragraph 6: seal to the face; note (11): vents that keep splash out | Splash goggles, with a face shield over them if needed |
| Dust | Covered by the general duty when particles fly | Paragraph 6: seal to the face; NIOSH: use engineering dust controls | Face-sealing goggles plus dust control at the source |
| Welding, cutting, brazing light | Filter lenses with a suitable shade number, 1910.133(a)(5) | Note (9): helmets and face shields go over spectacles or goggles; note (12): pick the darkest shade that still lets you do the task | Filter lenses, with a helmet worn over primary eye protection; see also [1910.252(b)(2)](https://www.osha.gov/eye-face-protection/standards) |
| Blood, droplets (infection) | Not in the 1910.133 list | NIOSH lists goggles, face shields, spectacles or full facepiece respirators | Chosen by the employer's hazard assessment |

## Worked example: choosing eye protection for a lab bench and a cut-off saw task

The two cases below are illustrations, not real incidents. They show how the rule text and Appendix B lead to a choice.

### Example 1: decanting a caustic cleaning solution at a lab bench

A technician who wears prescription glasses pours a caustic solution between containers.

1. Hazard: caustic liquid, which is named in 1910.133(a)(1), so eye protection is required.
2. Type: splash protection sealed against the face (Appendix B, paragraph 6), with vents that keep splash out (note 11). That points to splash goggles rather than spectacles.
3. Prescription: the goggles must fit over the technician's glasses without moving either set of lenses (1910.133(a)(3)).
4. Bigger pours: a face shield may be added, but only over the goggles (note 3).
5. Compliance: the goggles must carry the manufacturer's mark (a)(4) and meet a listed Z87.1 edition or (b)(2).

### Example 2: cutting concrete block with a cut-off saw on a construction site

A laborer on a construction site cuts concrete block with a cut-off saw. 29 CFR 1926.102 applies.

1. Hazard: flying particles. NIOSH names cement chips among the particles that cause most eye injuries. Side protection is required (1926.102, same wording as 1910.133(a)(2)).
2. Dust: Appendix B advises protection that seals to the face. Face-sealing goggles handle both the chips and the dust. NIOSH also points to engineering controls such as dust controls.
3. Severe exposure: a face shield goes over the goggles, never in place of them.
4. Fit: 1926.102 requires protectors that fit snugly, are reasonably comfortable, don't unduly interfere with movement, and can be easily cleaned and disinfected.
5. Head protection is a separate decision. See [hard hat types and classes](/blog/hard-hat-types-and-classes).

## How should safety glasses be inspected, cleaned and replaced?

Inspect, clean and maintain safety glasses at regular intervals so they keep giving the protection required. That is how [Appendix B](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910SubpartIAppB) describes what compliance with 1910.132(a) and (b) involves.

- Fit: Appendix B paragraph 5 says poorly fitting PPE "will not afford the necessary protection." NIOSH says to adjust eyewear for coverage, comfort and adequate peripheral vision.
- Cleaning: [1926.102](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.102) requires protectors that are easily cleanable and capable of being disinfected. Note (8) says fogging may call for frequent cleaning.
- Reassessment: Appendix B paragraph 7 asks the safety officer to reassess hazards and the suitability of the PPE already chosen when equipment or processes change, and to review accident records.

None of the federal text used for this guide sets a replacement interval in months or years. A pair that no longer fits, seals or stays clean enough to see through has stopped meeting these criteria. The pre-use checklist on this page turns these points into checks.

## Who pays for safety glasses?

OSHA's [standards page](https://www.osha.gov/eye-face-protection/standards) lists the Employer Payment for Personal Protective Equipment final rule (72:64341-64430, November 15, 2007). The [interpretation index](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/interlinking/standards/1910.133/standard_interpretations) lists a 05/14/2007 letter on "Employer liability and payment requirements for prescription protective eyewear." The text of the payment rule and its conditions isn't in the material this guide was built from, so this guide doesn't summarize it. Read the final rule and that letter, or ask OSHA, before deciding who pays for a particular pair.

## What to check at your workplace

Use these checks against the federal rules, then confirm anything specific to your site with your safety officer, your OSHA area office or a competent person.

- Confirm which rule covers you: 1910.133 for general industry or 1926.102 for construction. Maritime work falls under other standards, such as 1915.153 and 1918.101, which this guide doesn't cover.
- If your state runs a State Plan, check its rule. OSHA says State Plans must be no less effective than OSHA's and may have different or stricter requirements.
- Make sure there's a hazard assessment that names the eye hazards for each task, including tasks where several hazards occur at once.
- Check every pair for a manufacturer's mark and documentation showing a listed Z87.1 edition.
- Check that side protection is in place wherever flying-object hazards exist.
- Give splash and dust tasks face-sealing goggles, not open spectacles.
- Give prescription wearers Rx safety eyewear or protection that fits over their glasses.
- Make sure face shields and welding helmets are always worn over spectacles or goggles.
- Use the pre-use checklist on this page at the start of each shift.

NIOSH runs free Health Hazard Evaluations for employees, unions and employers who want an outside look at workplace hazards ([NIOSH eye safety page](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ppe/eye-safety/index.html)).
