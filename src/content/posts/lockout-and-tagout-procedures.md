Lockout and tagout procedures are the steps an employer sets out so that a machine is shut down, cut off from every energy source, locked or tagged, and confirmed safe before anyone services it. In US general industry, the rule is OSHA's [29 CFR 1910.147, The control of hazardous energy (lockout/tagout)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147). It covers servicing and maintenance where "the unexpected energization or start up of the machines or equipment, or release of stored energy could cause injury to employees."

OSHA also publishes an eight-step [sample lockout procedure in Appendix A](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147AppA). That appendix is optional. The standard's own note says "Nothing in the appendix adds to or detracts from any of the requirements of this section." This guide keeps the two apart: what the standard requires, and what OSHA offers as a model.

## What is lockout/tagout (LOTO) and what does "lockout" mean?

Lockout/tagout is OSHA's term for controlling hazardous energy during servicing and maintenance. OSHA's [hazardous energy overview](https://www.osha.gov/SLTC/controlhazardousenergy/) lists "electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical, thermal, or other sources" as energy that can hurt workers. The injuries it names include electrocution, burns, crushing, amputation and fractures.

The definitions in [1910.147(b)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147) are short:

- **Lockout** is "the placement of a lockout device on an energy isolating device, in accordance with an established procedure, ensuring that the energy isolating device and the equipment being controlled cannot be operated until the lockout device is removed."
- **Tagout** is placing a tagout device on an energy isolating device under an established procedure, "to indicate that the energy isolating device and the equipment being controlled may not be operated until the tagout device is removed."

The difference is in the verbs: a lock means the equipment *cannot* be operated, while a tag says it *may not* be.

### What counts as an energy isolating device?

An energy isolating device is a mechanical device that physically prevents energy from being transmitted or released. The standard's examples are a manually operated circuit breaker, a disconnect switch, a line valve and a block. It also states: "Push buttons, selector switches and other control circuit type devices are not energy isolating devices." Pressing stop on a control panel does not isolate a machine.

## What is the difference between a lockout device and a tagout device?

A lockout device physically holds the isolating device in a safe position. A tagout device is a warning that relies on people obeying it.

| | Lockout device | Tagout device |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition in [1910.147(b)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147) | Uses "a positive means such as a lock, either key or combination type" to hold the isolating device safe | "A prominent warning device, such as a tag and a means of attachment" |
| Examples named | Locks, blank flanges, bolted slip blinds | A tag fastened securely to the isolating device |
| When it may be used | Required where the device is capable of being locked out | On devices that can't be locked out, or on lockable ones if full employee protection is demonstrated |

A device is "capable of being locked out" if it has a hasp or other attachment point for a lock, or a built-in locking mechanism. It also counts if lockout can be done without dismantling, rebuilding or replacing the device, or permanently changing its energy control capability.

### Using tags on a device that could take a lock

The standard sets conditions for using a tag where a lock would fit. The tag goes "at the same location that the lockout device would have been attached." The employer must show that the tagout program is as safe as lockout. That means meeting every tagout-related provision plus additional measures, such as "removal of an isolating circuit element, blocking of a controlling switch, opening of an extra disconnecting device, or the removal of a valve handle." [Appendix A](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147AppA) adds that tagout brings additional training and more rigorous periodic inspections.

## When does OSHA's lockout/tagout standard (29 CFR 1910.147) apply?

1910.147 applies to servicing and maintenance in general industry where an employee could be hurt by unexpected startup or a release of stored energy. "Servicing and/or maintenance" includes "constructing, installing, setting up, adjusting, inspecting, modifying, and maintaining," as well as lubrication, cleaning, unjamming, adjustments and tool changes where the worker may be exposed to that hazard.

The rule does not reach:

- **Cord-and-plug equipment**, where unplugging controls the hazard *and* the plug stays under the exclusive control of the employee doing the work ([1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)).
- **Minor servicing during normal production** that is "routine, repetitive, and integral to the use of equipment for production," but only if alternative protective measures give effective protection. That's how OSHA's [enforcement directive STD 01-05-019](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) reads it.

Other industries have their own standards. OSHA's [hazardous energy standards page](https://www.osha.gov/control-hazardous-energy/standards) lists 1926.417 "Lockout and tagging of circuits" for construction, 1918.64 for powered conveyors in maritime work, and 1910.269 and 1910.261 for power generation and for pulp and paper mills. It also notes that State Plans "may have different or more stringent requirements." If you're in a state-plan state, check your state's rule as well.

## What must a hazardous energy control program include?

The employer must set up a program "consisting of energy control procedures, employee training and periodic inspections," under [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147). The procedures must "clearly and specifically outline the scope, purpose, authorization, rules, and techniques" for controlling hazardous energy and the means to enforce compliance. They must also include specific requirements for testing that locks, tags and other measures actually work.

OSHA's [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) tells inspectors to ask for three documents: the written procedures, certification of periodic inspections, and certification of training. A procedure must name the types of energy to be controlled. A common procedure shared by several machines must identify the equipment "at least by type and location."

### The eight-condition exception to writing a procedure

The note to 1910.147(c)(4)(i) lets an employer skip documenting a procedure for one machine only when all eight of these conditions exist:

1. No stored or residual energy, and no reaccumulation of it after shutdown, that could endanger employees.
2. A single energy source that can be readily identified and isolated.
3. Isolating and locking out that source completely deenergizes and deactivates the machine.
4. The machine is isolated and locked out during servicing or maintenance.
5. A single lockout device achieves a locked-out condition.
6. The lockout device is under the exclusive control of the authorized employee doing the work.
7. The work does not create hazards for other employees.
8. The employer has had no accidents involving unexpected activation or reenergization of that machine during servicing or maintenance.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A plant wants to skip the written procedure for a conveyor that has one electrical disconnect. Its maintenance practice, however, also puts a second lock on the downstream equipment the conveyor feeds. That fails condition 5, because one lockout device no longer achieves the locked-out condition. If the conveyor also had a raised member or a spring that stayed loaded after shutdown, condition 1 would fail too. Either way, the procedure has to be written.

## What are the steps in lockout and tagout procedures?

The steps below follow OSHA's [Appendix A sample procedure](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147AppA). Each step is paired with the matching requirement in [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147), which says the application of control must be done "in the following sequence." OSHA's [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) sums up that sequence as shutdown, equipment isolation, lockout/tagout application, release of stored energy and verification of isolation.

| Step | Appendix A sample (optional) | What 1910.147 requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Notify | Tell affected employees the machine must be shut down and locked out | Affected employees must be notified before controls are applied |
| 2. Prepare | Authorized employee identifies the type and magnitude of energy, its hazards and how to control it | Before shutdown, the authorized employee "shall have knowledge of the type and magnitude of the energy" and its hazards and controls |
| 3. Shut down | Use the normal stopping procedure (stop button, open switch, close valve) | Shutdown is part of the required sequence |
| 4. Isolate | Deactivate the energy isolating device(s) | All isolating devices needed must be "physically located and operated" to isolate the machine |
| 5. Lock out | Apply assigned individual locks | Only the authorized employees doing the work apply locks or tags |
| 6. Release stored energy | Dissipate or restrain energy in capacitors, springs, elevated members, flywheels, hydraulic systems, and air, gas, steam or water pressure, by grounding, repositioning, blocking or bleeding down | Release of stored energy is part of the required sequence |
| 7. Verify | Check no personnel are exposed, then operate the normal controls or test to make sure the equipment won't run | Procedures must contain specific requirements for testing effectiveness |
| 8. Locked out | The machine is now locked out | — |

Appendix A also cautions you to return the operating controls to neutral or "off" after verifying. OSHA says complex systems "may need" more comprehensive procedures than this sample.

## How do you release lockout/tagout and restore equipment to service?

Release reverses the sequence with its own checks. [Appendix A](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147AppA) suggests five steps:

1. Check the machine and the area around it: nonessential items removed, components operationally intact.
2. Check that all employees are safely positioned or out of the area.
3. Verify the controls are in neutral.
4. Remove the lockout devices and reenergize. Removing some forms of blocking may require reenergizing first.
5. Notify affected employees that the work is done and the machine is ready.

The binding rules in [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147) are:

- Affected employees must be told the devices are off after removal and before the machine is started.
- Under (e)(3), "each lockout or tagout device shall be removed from each energy isolating device by the employee who applied the device." If that person isn't available, the device may be removed under the employer's direction. The specific procedures and training for this must be developed, documented and built into the program, and the employer must show the method gives equivalent safety. The standard lists the minimum elements of that procedure, so read the full text before drafting one.
- Under (f)(1), locks may come off temporarily to test or position a machine. OSHA's [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) says this is allowed "ONLY during the limited time necessary." Afterwards the equipment is deenergized and locked or tagged out again.

## What requirements apply to LOTO locks and tags?

Lockout devices must use a positive means, such as a key or combination lock, to hold the isolating device safe ([1910.147(b)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)). Tags "must be legible and understandable by all authorized employees, affected employees, and all other employees" in the area. Where a tag can't be fixed directly to the device, it goes "as close as safely possible," where anyone trying to operate the device will see it straight away.

Each worker uses their own device. The [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) states: "No employee may affix the personal lockout/tagout device of another employee." For how colors and symbols work on workplace signs generally, see [safety signals, signs and colours](/blog/safety-signals-signs-symbols-colours).

## Who can apply and remove locks: authorized vs affected employees?

Only authorized employees apply locks and tags. Under [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147), lockout or tagout "shall be performed only by the authorized employees who are performing the servicing or maintenance." An authorized employee is the person who locks or tags out a machine to service or maintain it. An affected employee "becomes an authorized employee when that employee's duties include performing servicing or maintenance."

The [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) recognizes three groups, each with different training:

- **Authorized employees:** apply and remove their own devices.
- **Affected employees:** defined in 1910.147(b) and notified before and after each lockout.
- **Other employees:** work in areas where procedures may be used and must be told not to restart locked or tagged equipment.

## How does group lockout, shift change or outside-contractor lockout work?

In a group lockout, every authorized employee still has personal protection. Under [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147), each one "shall affix a personal lockout or tagout device to the group lockout device, group lockbox, or comparable mechanism" when starting work, and removes it when they stop. The procedure must also let the authorized employee in charge check the exposure status of each group member.

OSHA's [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019) gives example group methods. It says these "are not intended to represent the only acceptable procedures":

- **Type B:** a lock or job-tag goes on each isolating device, the keys or tabs go in a lockbox, and each worker puts a personal lock or tag on the box.
- **Type C:** a master lockbox holds the keys. Each group's principal authorized employee locks the master box and puts their key in a satellite lockbox, which each group member locks.
- **Type D:** for work over more than one shift, a job-lock secures the master lockbox first. The job-lock may have multiple keys only if they are in the sole possession of the principal authorized employees, one on each shift.

For sophisticated and complex equipment, such as process equipment in petroleum refining, petroleum production and chemical production, the directive allows an alternative to normal group procedures only "if the company documentation justifies it." The factors to evaluate include the physical size of the equipment, how inaccessible the isolating devices are, the number of employees and of isolating devices, and how components and systems depend on each other. Within such an alternative, a work permit or "master tag" system, with each employee personally signing on and off, combined with verification of hazardous energy control, work procedures and walk-through, is an acceptable approach.

### Shift changes and contractors

Shift changes need "specific procedures" for the orderly transfer of protection between off-going and oncoming employees. When outside servicing personnel are brought in, the on-site employer and the contractor "shall inform each other of their respective lockout or tagout procedures." The on-site employer must also make sure its own staff understand and comply with the contractor's restrictions ([1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)).

**Worked example (illustrative).** A boiler overhaul runs across several shifts. Following the directive's Type D model, each isolating device is locked and the keys go in a master lockbox. A job-lock goes on the box first. The principal authorized employee on each shift holds one job-lock key, kept in that person's sole possession, and adds a personal lock. Each crew member adds their own lock when arriving and takes it off when leaving. Each one also verifies that the hazardous energy has been made safe. Because the job-lock never comes off between shifts, the transfer stays secure.

## How often must energy control procedures be inspected, and what training is required?

Each energy control procedure must be inspected "at least annually" ([1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)). Under 1910.147(c)(6)(i), as described in OSHA's [directive](https://osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-05-019), the inspector is an authorized employee other than those using the procedure. The inspection includes at least a demonstration of the procedure.

- **Lockout:** the inspector reviews responsibilities with each authorized employee. Group meetings count.
- **Tagout:** the review covers each authorized *and* affected employee, plus the tagout training elements.
- **Certification:** must name the machine or equipment, the date, the employees included and the person who inspected.

Training must give employees the knowledge and skills for safe application, use and removal of energy controls. Authorized employees learn to recognize hazardous energy sources, the type and magnitude of energy in the workplace, and how to isolate it. All other employees in the area are told about the procedure and the ban on restarting locked or tagged equipment. Retraining is required when job assignments change, when machines or processes change in a way that presents a new hazard, or when procedures change. Where tagout is permitted, the directive says training of authorized, affected and other employees must also cover the tagout provisions in 1910.147(c)(7)(ii) and (d)(4)(iii).

## When do OSHA's electrical lockout rules (1910.333) apply instead of, or as well as, 1910.147?

OSHA lists several electrical rules alongside 1910.147, but the sources used for this guide don't spell out where 1910.333 replaces 1910.147 and where both apply. OSHA's [standards page](https://www.osha.gov/control-hazardous-energy/standards) includes 1910.333 "Selection and use of work practices" and 1910.306 under Subpart S (Electrical), plus 1910.269 for electric power generation, transmission and distribution. It also points to directive STD 01-16-007, Electrical Safety-Related Work Practices. Read 1910.333 and that directive before planning electrical lockouts, and note that construction electrical work has its own rule, 1926.417.

The consensus standard ANSI/ASSE Z244.1 is not an OSHA regulation. OSHA notes that complying with it "does not ensure compliance with the OSHA Lockout/Tagout Standard."

## What to check at your workplace

Use the checklist on this page at the machine and against your documents. Start with these points:

- Does every machine have a written procedure, or do all eight exception conditions exist for it?
- Are locks used wherever the isolating device can take one? Where tags are used instead, is the full-protection demonstration on file?
- Does each procedure name its energy types, its isolation points, how stored energy is released and how isolation is verified?
- Was each procedure inspected in the last year by someone other than its users, and is the certification complete?
- Are there written methods for group lockout, shift handover, contractors and removing an absent worker's lock?

This guide is based on OSHA's own copy of 1910.147. Compare it with the current text on eCFR before relying on any paragraph. For questions about your own equipment, contact [OSHA](/blog/what-does-osha-stand-for) or your state plan, or ask a competent person who knows the machines.
