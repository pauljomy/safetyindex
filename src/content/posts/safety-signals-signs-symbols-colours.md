In a US workplace, “safety signals” come from three different OSHA systems, and mixing them up is the most common mistake. Signs and tags are covered by [29 CFR 1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145). Chemical label pictograms are covered by the Hazard Communication standard, 29 CFR 1910.1200.

This guide covers US OSHA rules only. Colours and symbols in the UK, EU and elsewhere follow other rules (see the section on that below).

| System | Where it's used | What the source says |
|---|---|---|
| Safety signs | Fixed signs warning of or instructing about hazards | Classes in the text: danger, caution, safety instruction ([1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)) |
| Accident prevention tags | Temporary tags on out-of-the-ordinary hazards | Signal word “Danger”, “Caution” or biological hazard; “Warning” optional ([1910.145(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)) |
| Chemical pictograms | Labels on hazardous chemical containers | Black symbol, white background, red frame set on a point ([OSHA pictograms](https://www.osha.gov/hazcom/pictograms)) |

## What are safety signals, and how is a warning sign different from a hazard symbol?

A **sign**, in OSHA's definition, is a surface prepared for the warning of, or safety instructions for, industrial workers or members of the public who may be exposed to hazards. News releases, safety posters and bulletins used for employee education are excluded ([1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)).

A **symbol** is the picture. The standard covers signs or symbols meant to indicate, and as far as possible define, specific hazards where failing to mark them may lead to injury or property damage. It is meant to cover all safety signs except those for streets, highways and railroads. It does not apply to plant bulletin boards or safety posters.

A hazard **pictogram** is something else: a symbol with a border on a chemical label, governed by the Hazard Communication standard. See the pictogram section below.

Two more points from the text. All new signs and replacements of old signs must follow the specifications. Wording should be easily read and concise, make a positive rather than negative suggestion, and be accurate in fact.

## What do danger, caution and warning mean on US safety signs?

| Word | Meaning in the OSHA text | Employee instruction |
|---|---|---|
| Danger | Danger signs indicate immediate danger and that special precautions are necessary | Employees must be instructed on this |
| Caution | Used only to warn against potential hazards or to caution against unsafe practices; indicates a possible hazard | Employees must be instructed on this |
| Warning (tags) | A level between “Caution” and “Danger”, used instead of the required “Caution” tag | Must still meet the general tag criteria |

The sign text also says there shall be no variation in the type of design of signs posted to warn of specific dangers and radiation hazards ([1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)).

The sign classes in the text I reviewed are danger, caution and safety instruction. “Warning” is defined in the tag rules, not as a separate sign class. Don't assume a sign headed “Warning” follows a particular OSHA colour. Check the current text.

## What do safety sign colours mean?

The colours below are only those in the 1910.145 text. Sign colours must match the safety colours in ANSI Z535.1 (or the older ANSI Z53.1-1967), incorporated by reference.

| Item | Colours in the standard |
|---|---|
| Danger sign | Red, black and white, as specified in the ANSI table |
| Caution sign | Yellow background; black panel with yellow letters; letters on the yellow background are black |
| Safety instruction sign | White background; green panel with white letters; letters on the white background are black |
| Slow-moving vehicle emblem | Fluorescent yellow-orange triangle with a dark red reflective border |

Safety instruction signs are for general instructions and suggestions about safety measures. The slow-moving vehicle emblem is for vehicles that by design move at 25 m.p.h. or less on public roads. It is not a clearance marker for wide machinery ([1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)).

Many web pages give a single colour chart (red, orange, yellow, blue, green). Treat those with care. They are often not from regulators, and they disagree with each other. This guide only states what the regulation text supports. The 1910.145 text I used has no OSHA meaning for blue.

## What do the hazard communication pictograms on chemical labels mean?

OSHA's [pictogram page](https://www.osha.gov/hazcom/pictograms) lists nine: flame over circle, flame, exploding bomb, skull and crossbones, corrosion, gas cylinder, health hazard, environment and exclamation mark.

Appendix C of 29 CFR 1910.1200 says a pictogram is a square set at a point, with a black hazard symbol on a white background and a red frame sufficiently wide to be clearly visible. A red frame without a symbol is not a pictogram and is not permitted on the label.

This guide doesn't list what hazard class each symbol stands for, because the pages I could cite don't spell that out. One match is confirmed: in [OSHA's 2012 interpretation letter](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2012-12-20), the flame over circle is the oxidizer symbol. For the rest, read Appendix C and the chemical's safety data sheet.

### What does the health hazard symbol mean?

“Health Hazard” is one of the nine pictograms OSHA lists. The pages I could cite don't say which hazard classes it covers. Rather than guess, check Appendix C of 1910.1200 and the label and safety data sheet of the chemical in front of you.

### Label rules people get wrong

The 2012 letter answers several practical questions:

- **Preprinted labels with three red frames.** Blank red frames are not allowed. Blacking out unused frames is compliant, and adding “See Adjacent Pictogram(s)” may reduce concern that the label was defaced.
- **Pictogram stickers.** Acceptable if they are on the label and sufficiently wide to be clearly visible.
- **DOT diamond labels.** The letter treats them as pictograms. OSHA says it intends to revise Appendix C.2.3.3 and, in the meantime, allows both DOT and HCS pictograms for the same hazard on the label.
- **Shipping boxes.** The immediate container must be labelled. An outside box or overpack needs an HCS label only if it is also the immediate container, or if it is an overpack because the immediate container has ruptured or is leaking.

The letter is from 2012. Check the current standard for any later changes.

For example, a shop buys labels with three empty red frames, but its product needs one pictogram. Following the letter, it fills one frame with the correct pictogram and blacks out the other two. It may add “See Adjacent Pictogram(s)” in the blacked-out area.

## What do first aid signs and “safe condition” symbols look like?

The OSHA sources I could use don't describe first aid sign designs, so this guide won't. The closest rule in the text is the safety instruction sign: white background, green panel, white letters, used for general safety instructions.

If you need a first aid or emergency sign for your site, ask OSHA or your state plan which standard applies. Some standards for specific hazards may carry their own sign requirements.

## Which signs are legally required, and what must employees be told?

1910.145 sets how signs and tags are designed and used. It doesn't list every place a sign is needed. Other OSHA standards may require tags or signs for particular hazards, and the tag rules say they apply to “the specific tagging requirements of other OSHA standards”.

What the text does require:

1. Employees are instructed that danger signs indicate immediate danger and that special precautions are necessary.
2. Employees are instructed that caution signs indicate a possible hazard against which proper precaution should be taken.
3. For tags, all employees are informed of the meaning of the various tags used throughout the workplace and what special precautions are necessary.
4. Signs have rounded or blunt corners and are free from sharp edges, burrs and splinters. Fastener heads must not create a hazard.

## How do accident prevention tags differ from signs?

Tags are for hazards that are out of the ordinary, unexpected or not readily apparent. They stay up until the hazard is eliminated or the operation is completed. Tags need not be used where signs, guarding or other positive means of protection are being used.

| Tag requirement | What 1910.145(f) says |
|---|---|
| Content | A signal word and a major message |
| Signal word | “Danger”, “Caution”, “Biological Hazard”, “BIOHAZARD” or the biological hazard symbol |
| Readability | Signal word readable from at least five feet (1.52 m), or more if the hazard warrants |
| Fixing | As close as safely possible to the hazard, by string, wire or adhesive that prevents loss or unintentional removal |
| Danger tag | Major hazards: immediate threat of death or serious injury |
| Caution tag | Minor hazards: non-immediate or potential hazard, or unsafe practice |
| Scope | Does not apply to construction or agriculture |

Examples of major messages in the text are “High Voltage”, “Close Clearance”, “Do Not Start” and “Do Not Use”.

The tag colour scheme is a recommendation. The standard says it does not mandate tag colours. OSHA recommends red for danger, yellow for caution, orange for warning, and fluorescent orange or orange-red for biological hazard ([1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145)).

For example, if a machine must not be started, a tag carries the signal word “Danger” and the major message “Do Not Start”. It sits on the machine with string, wire or adhesive. A worker standing five feet away must be able to read “Danger”. It stays on until the hazard is gone.

## How do US signs differ from UK and ISO 7010 signs?

They follow different rules. I had no HSE, ISO 7010 or ANSI Z535 pages to cite, so this guide doesn't describe UK or ISO colours and shapes. If you work outside the US, or sign a site that follows both, check your national regulator. Don't borrow the OSHA colours above.

## How do I run a 10-minute toolbox talk on safety signs?

Use your own site's signs, tags and a couple of chemical labels as props.

1. **Minutes 0–2: three systems.** Say: “Signs, tags and chemical labels each follow a different rule. Today we'll tell them apart.”
2. **Minutes 2–4: danger and caution.** Say: “Danger means immediate danger and special precautions are necessary. Caution means a possible hazard, so take proper precaution.” Walk to one of each.
3. **Minutes 4–6: tags.** Show a tag. Ask: “What's the signal word? What's the major message? Who may remove it?” Point out that tags stay until the hazard is eliminated.
4. **Minutes 6–8: pictograms.** Show a label. Point out the black symbol, white background and red frame set on a point. Ask who can find the safety data sheet.
5. **Minutes 8–10: check understanding.** Ask each person to name one sign near their work and what it tells them to do. Note anyone who can't, and follow up. If a sign sends people to protective equipment, point them to the [PPE guide](/blog/what-does-ppe-stand-for).

## What should I check at my own workplace?

- Read the current [29 CFR 1910.145](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-J/section-1910.145) on eCFR. It is authoritative but unofficial.
- If you are under a state OSHA plan, check for added requirements.
- Check that any new or replaced signs match the specifications, and that corners and fasteners are safe.
- Confirm that danger signs and caution signs are used as defined, and that wording is short, accurate and positive.
- Make sure every employee has been told what danger signs, caution signs and your tags mean.
- Check that tags are readable at five feet, firmly attached, and removed only when the hazard is gone.
- Inspect chemical labels: no blank red frames, and pictograms visible.
- Check whether other standards for your hazards (for example radiation or confined spaces) add their own sign rules.

For questions on your site, ask OSHA, your state plan, or a qualified safety professional.
